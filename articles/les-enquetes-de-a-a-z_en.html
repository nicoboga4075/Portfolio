Surveys from A to Z

Customer satisfaction, social barometer, quality of work life... Companies regularly ask their customers and their employees for feedback to understand them better and, above all, to make well-founded decisions: where to invest, which team to support, which action to launch first. A well-run survey is a true decision support tool: it turns the opinions gathered into measurable indicators, tracked over time and comparable from one department to another. Behind every questionnaire thus lie standard indicators, several data collection modes and a process in several steps, up to the reporting that guides the action plan. This article goes through them, based on what I learned at Soft Concept, a Lyon-based publisher and host of survey software.

The key indicators

The results of a satisfaction survey rest on KPIs (Key Performance Indicators). They are tracked in real time during data collection and allow the results to be compared from one wave to the next, from one department to another or with other companies.

The CSAT (Customer Satisfaction Score) measures satisfaction with a specific interaction: a purchase, a delivery, a conversation with support. A question such as "How satisfied are you with...?" is asked on a scale from 1 to 5, and the score is the share of respondents who are satisfied or very satisfied (4 or 5). A CSAT of 80% means that 8 respondents out of 10 picked one of these two ratings.

The NPS (Net Promoter Score), created by Fred Reichheld and Bain & Company in 2003, measures loyalty rather than satisfaction in the moment. The question is always the same: "How likely are you to recommend our company to a friend?", rated from 0 to 10. Respondents are classified as promoters (9 or 10), passives (7 or 8) and detractors (0 to 6), and the NPS is the share of promoters minus the share of detractors. It therefore ranges from -100 to +100: with 50% promoters, 30% passives and 20% detractors, the NPS is +30.

The CES (Customer Effort Score) measures the effort a customer had to make to get what they wanted: solve a problem, complete a procedure, find a piece of information. They are asked, for example, whether it was easy to handle their request, on an agreement scale, often from 1 to 7. The lower the effort, the more likely the customer is to come back.

Finally, the response rate, the share of the people invited who answered, measures how reliable the results are: the higher it is, the more faithfully the scores represent the population surveyed.


Indicator Typical question Scale Calculation
CSAT How satisfied are you with...? 1 to 5 Share of ratings 4 and 5
NPS Would you recommend... to a friend? 0 to 10 % of promoters (9-10) minus % of detractors (0-6)
CES Was it easy to...? 1 to 7, most often Average rating, or share of "easy" answers

Survey or poll?

The two words are often used for one another, yet they describe two distinct approaches. A survey is any collection of information from people through a questionnaire. It can be exhaustive, when the whole population concerned is asked (every employee of a company for a social barometer, every customer delivered in the month), in which case the results describe that population directly, all the more faithfully as the response rate is high.

A poll (a sample survey) is a survey of a sample, a part of the population chosen to represent it, so as to draw conclusions about the whole. The sample is built either at random (probability sampling, where every person has a known chance of being drawn) or with the quota method, the most common one in French polling institutes, which reproduces the structure of the population along a few criteria (gender, age, occupation, region). The results are then weighted: the answers are adjusted to correct the gaps between the sample obtained and the target population.

A poll always comes with a margin of error, which depends mostly on the size of the sample, whatever the size of the population. For a random sample of 1,000 people, a result of 50% is known to within about 3 points, at a 95% confidence level: the true value very likely lies between 47% and 53%. Halving this margin takes a sample four times larger. In France, the publication of election polls is governed by the law of July 19, 1977 and overseen by the Commission des sondages.

The different types of survey

Depending on who is asked and about what, there are several families of surveys:

  • the customer satisfaction survey, either right after an interaction or a few weeks later, to step back
  • the social barometer, which regularly measures the internal climate and the engagement of employees
  • the quality of work life survey, about working conditions, work-life balance or management
  • the HSE (health, safety, environment) survey, about the perception of safety at work
  • internal surveys, such as those of the Pragma Internal Surveys platform, about relationship quality or teamwork
  • surveys of a panel, a group of people recruited to be invited several times, such as the panel I built for the MNT

A survey can also be a one-off or come back in waves, at regular intervals, to compare the results of wave N with those of wave N-1 and follow how they change.

The data collection modes

The data collection mode, how the questionnaire reaches the respondents and how their answers are gathered, matters as much as the questions themselves: it sets who can answer, how much each answer costs and how candid the answers are. Professionals often call each mode by its acronym. At Soft Concept, each one has its software, and every answer lands in the same database to be analyzed together.

The web

The online survey, or CAWI (Computer Assisted Web Interviewing), is now the most widespread mode. The respondent gets a link by email or text message, scans a QR code or follows a link on a website, and fills in the questionnaire alone, on a computer, a tablet or a phone. It is the most economical mode, since its cost stays almost flat whatever the number of answers, and the fastest: the results come in in real time. The software handles the filters and question skips, the random order of the answer options and the input checks (a mandatory answer, a value within a range). Alone in front of the screen, the respondent also speaks more freely on sensitive topics. The web addresses people who are online and whose address is known, and well-spaced invitations keep the response rate high. Example tools: Net-Survey at Soft Concept, and among the best known Google Forms, Microsoft Forms, SurveyMonkey, Qualtrics and Typeform.

Mobile

Mobile data collection puts the questionnaire in an app installed on the tablet or smartphone of an interviewer, or of a respondent in self-service: at a trade show, at the exit of a shop, in a reception hall, on a building site or during an on-site audit. When an interviewer asks the questions face to face, it is called CAPI (Computer Assisted Personal Interviewing). Its main strength is to work offline: the answers are stored on the device and synchronized as soon as the network is back. Mobile also lets a photo, a signature or the GPS position be attached to an answer, which makes it a quality control tool as much as a survey one. Example tools: Mobi-Survey at Soft Concept, and among the best known KoboToolbox, SurveyCTO and the Qualtrics offline app.

The phone

The telephone survey, or CATI (Computer Assisted Telephone Interviewing), relies on a team of interviewers who call the respondents and enter their answers on screen, following a questionnaire that adapts to each answer. The software hands out the numbers to call, schedules the callbacks at the agreed times, records the outcome of each call (absent, appointment, refusal) and tracks the quotas in real time to move on to another profile as soon as a quota is reached; a supervisor can listen to the interviews to check their quality. The phone reaches people who are rarely online and lets the interviewer follow up, explain a question or dig into an open answer. Each answer takes interviewer time, and neutrally worded questions limit the social desirability bias, the tendency to give the answer one finds the most acceptable when talking to someone. Example tools: Cati-Web at Soft Concept, and among the best known Voxco and Askia.

Paper

The paper questionnaire, or PAPI (Paper And Pencil Interviewing), remains valuable to reach respondents away from screens: hospital patients, travelers, factory or building site workers, elderly people. The questionnaires are handed out, left in a ballot box or sent by mail with a return envelope, then scanned: optical recognition (OMR, Optical Mark Recognition) reads the ticked boxes, and ICR (Intelligent Character Recognition) the handwritten answers, which replaces manual re-entry with fast and reliable processing. For the reading to be reliable, the questionnaire must be designed for it: clear boxes, marks in the corners of the page and an identifier on each sheet. Filters and tracking apply once the questionnaires are read, and paper opens the survey to people away from digital tools. Example tools: OMR Manager at Soft Concept, and among the best known Remark Office OMR and TeleForm.

Mixed mode

Combining several modes in the same survey, mixed mode, reaches each respondent through the channel that suits them: the web for office staff, paper or a tablet for field teams, the phone to follow up on the people still expected. The questionnaire then stays the same from one mode to another, and the results are compared with care, since the same respondent may nuance their answers depending on whether they are alone in front of a screen or facing an interviewer.

The steps of a survey

A survey always follows the same cycle, with support available before, during and after it:

  1. The scoping

    The objectives, the population to survey, the sample or panel and the schedule are set. It is also the time to choose the indicators to track and the data collection mode or modes.

  2. The design of the questionnaire

    The questions are written (closed, on a scale, open-ended to collect verbatims), filters are planned so that each respondent gets the questions that concern them, and demographic questions (department, management, seniority...) are added to break down the results. The questionnaire is then customized (template, logo, messages) and tested before the launch. Example tools: EthnoForms and Net-Survey at Soft Concept, and among the best known Google Forms, SurveyMonkey and Qualtrics.

  3. The data collection

    The email addresses are imported, the invitations are sent, then the reminders to the people still expected, or letters with access codes and a QR code are printed. Meanwhile, the participation tracking shows the response rate in real time, until the survey closes.

  4. The processing and analysis

    The answers are cleaned, then analyzed: frequency tables, cross-tabulations by the demographic questions, calculation of the indicators and analysis of the open-ended answers, now assisted by AI. Example tools: Ethnos and EthnosAI at Soft Concept, and among the best known Excel, IBM SPSS Statistics and R.

  5. The reporting

    The results are presented in a report or a web reporting application: dashboards, data viz charts, results by category, comparison with the previous wave and a consolidated report for a group of several entities. This reporting is what turns the answers into an action plan. Example tools: Web-Reports at Soft Concept, and among the best known Power BI, Tableau and Looker Studio.

CSAT, NPS and CES complement each other: the first judges an interaction, the second the relationship over time and the third how simple a journey is. A good survey picks the indicator that answers its question, the data collection mode that reaches its target and a reporting that makes people want to act. Method does the rest, from the scoping to the last wave.
